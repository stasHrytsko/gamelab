Most ideas sound better in a document than they feel when you can actually touch them. A sentence can hide a slow interaction, an obvious choice or a payoff that never arrives.

Building small prototypes made that gap impossible to ignore.

## A clear rule is not always an interesting decision

Some mechanics are easy to explain and still produce only one sensible move. The player understands the rule, but there is nothing to weigh. That is useful to discover early, before adding levels, progression and polish around an empty centre.

## The first minute matters more than the tenth level

A prototype does not need a lot of content. It needs enough situations to reveal whether the central choice changes. One teaching level, one honest decision and one level that tries to break the idea often tell me more than a long campaign.

## Payoff needs to be visible

A clever move that only changes an internal number does not feel clever. The result should be readable in a second: space opens, a route appears, several objects react, or a risky decision visibly pays back.

## Writing the kill criterion helps

Before building, I now try to write down what would make me stop. It keeps the test honest. Otherwise every weak result can be explained away as a level problem, a presentation problem or something to fix later.

I still like documents. They make an idea precise. But the prototype is where the idea starts answering back.
